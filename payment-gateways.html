How Payment Gateways Work: From Checkout to Settlement

You order a pair of headphones online. The website asks for your card details, thinks for two seconds and says “Payment successful.” The headphones are on their way. The money, however, has a longer journey ahead of it.

Those two seconds hide a surprisingly crowded conversation between systems you rarely think about. Your bank, the seller's financial partners, a card network, fraud checks and a payment platform may all be involved. And the cheerful success screen doesn't necessarily mean the seller already has the money in its bank account.

Let's follow a fictional ₹2,000 online card purchase from checkout to the merchant's final records. Card payment arrangements differ across countries and providers, so this is a simplified model of a common card flow, not a universal transaction diagram.

First, meet the parties involved

The checkout page might display the logo of one payment company, but several roles sit behind the screen.

  • Customer or cardholder: The person buying the headphones.
  • Merchant: The online shop selling them.
  • Payment gateway: The technology that securely collects and transmits payment information or payment requests between the checkout and payment processing systems.
  • Payment processor: The provider that processes transaction messages and connects the merchant side to the relevant payment infrastructure.
  • Acquirer or acquiring bank: The institution that enables merchants to accept card payments, directly or through a payment-facilitator arrangement.
  • Card network: A network such as Visa or Mastercard that routes card transaction messages and supports clearing and settlement.
  • Issuer or issuing bank: The bank or institution that issued the customer's card and makes the authorization decision.

These are roles, not always separate companies. A single payment provider may bundle gateway, processing and related to acquiring services. The contractual structure matters, especially when comparing products marketed simply as “payment gateways.”

A payment gateway is not the same as the bank

A gateway is commonly the used at checkout technology layer. It might provide hosted payment fields, an API, tokenization, fraud screening hooks and transaction status notifications. It helps the merchant move a payment request into the processing chain securely.

The issuer, meanwhile, decides whether to authorize a card transaction. The card network generally routes the authorization message. The acquirer and processor operate on the merchant side of that exchange.

So when someone says, “The gateway approved my card,” that's convenient shorthand. Technically, the authorization response usually originates from the issuer or another authorized decisioning participant in the payment system and travels back through the chain.

Step 1: The customer clicks Pay

Our customer adds the headphones to the cart and enters card details in a secure checkout interface. In a modern integration, sensitive fields may be hosted by the payment provider rather than handled directly by the merchant's own server. That can reduce the merchant's exposure to cardholder data, though it does not automatically eliminate every compliance obligation.

The gateway or payment service validates the request format and sends the necessary information toward the processor and acquirer. The request can carry the amount, currency, merchant identifiers, transaction information and payment credentials or tokens.

A merchant should avoid treating an ordinary browser redirect or a “success” message on the front end as definitive proof of payment. Server-to-server confirmations, verified webhooks and transaction lookups are typically important for reliable fulfillment.

Step 2: Authentication and authorization are different

Here's a common source of confusion. Authentication asks, in effect, whether the person attempting the payment is the legitimate cardholder. Authorization asks whether this transaction may proceed.

Some online payments involve additional cardholder authentication, such as 3-D Secure, where the issuer or its authentication service may request a challenge or perform a frictionless check. The exact experience depends on region, merchant configuration, issuer decisions and applicable rules. A one-time password screen isn't a universal feature of card payments.

The authorization request then travels from the used by the merchant processor or acquirer, through the relevant card network, to the issuer.

The issuer checks information such as card status, available credit or funds, transaction risk and applicable controls. It can approve or decline the request. An approval may place a hold or reduce the cardholder's available spending capacity, depending on the card and transaction type.

The issuer sends its response back along the chain. If approved, the merchant can usually proceed with the order, subject to the chosen capture flow and any other conditions.

Step 3: Authorization does not equal settlement

Imagine your friend says, “I've reserved the seats. I haven't paid the venue yet.” Authorization is roughly that first stage: the system has received permission to proceed with the transaction, but the final movement of money is not necessarily complete.

Some businesses authorize and capture immediately, which is common for simple e-commerce purchases. Others authorize first and capture later, for instance when goods ship. Hotels and rental services may use preauthorizations and adjustments, subject to network and provider rules.

Capture is the used by the merchant action that signals a previously authorized payment should be finalized for clearing and settlement, within permitted timing and amount rules. An authorization can expire or be reversed; a capture can fail or require exception handling. So developers must model payment states carefully rather than treating every approval as an irreversible completed payment.

A helpful lifecycle sketch is:

Checkout → Authentication if required → Authorization → Capture → Clearing → Settlement → Merchant payout/reconciliation

Different providers combine or rename certain states, and some processes overlap. This diagram is a conceptual guide, not a set of guaranteed API state names.

Step 4: Clearing tells everyone what is owed

Once a transaction has been captured and submitted, clearing involves exchanging the financial transaction details needed to record obligations among the relevant participants. The transaction can be grouped with many others, with network rules determining how they are processed.

Consider the distinction: authorization answered whether the issuer would approve ₹2,000. Clearing communicates the completed financial transaction and the information needed to post and settle it.

This is where adjustments, fees, currency conversion and other specific to each transaction details can matter. The amount ultimately paid out to the merchant may differ from the original purchase amount because of provider fees, reserves, refunds, chargebacks or other agreed deductions.

Step 5: Settlement moves funds between institutions

Settlement refers to discharging the financial obligations created by clearing. Depending on the scheme and geography, this involves transfers or net settlement positions among issuers, networks, acquirers and their settlement arrangements.

Our customer may see the ₹2,000 purchase on their card account while the merchant receives a payout later. Those are connected events, but they are not identical events.

The acquirer or payment service then makes funds available to the merchant according to its payout schedule and contract. A provider might offer the following day payouts, delayed payouts or another arrangement. There is no universal promise that money arrives in a merchant's account within a particular number of hours.

This distinction becomes important when a business has to pay suppliers today but receives card proceeds later. Payment processing is not only a checkout engineering problem; it is a cash flow problem too.

What actually reaches the merchant?

Suppose our fictional merchant sells the headphones for ₹2,000 and incurs ₹40 in combined processing charges purely for illustration. Ignoring taxes, refunds, reserves and any other adjustments, its net proceeds would be ₹1,960.

That arithmetic is simple. Real reconciliation isn't always.

The merchant might see one row for the sale in its order system, another transaction ID in the payment provider's dashboard, a fee entry in the settlement report and a single bank credit representing hundreds of purchases. Each system describes a different part of the same journey.

A thoughtfully designed finance workflow matches:

  • Order ID to the correct customer purchase.
  • Payment transaction ID to authorization, capture and any reversal or refund events.
  • Settlement or balance transaction ID to fees and gross and net amounts.
  • Payout ID to the actual bank deposit.

If 50 orders total ₹1,00,000, that does not automatically mean the next bank deposit will be ₹1,00,000. Fees, transaction timing, failed captures, refunds and payouts from other periods can change the number. Reconciliation explains the difference.

Why payment states need careful engineering

A checkout system is a distributed system: different services exchange messages, networks time out and acknowledgments can arrive late. A merchant might receive a timeout after sending a payment request, without knowing whether the transaction actually succeeded.

Blindly retrying that request can risk a duplicate charge or duplicate order. Many payment APIs provide idempotency keys, allowing a correctly designed client to retry an operation without creating a second independent result. Developers still need to read the provider's specific semantics and expiration rules.

Webhooks can also arrive more than once or out of order. Systems should verify signatures, process events idempotently and treat the payment provider's authoritative state as more reliable than the customer's browser screen.

This is where the friendly “Pay now” button reveals its true personality: it is the front door to a distributed financial workflow.

Security is not one checkbox

Because payment data is valuable, payment systems need multiple defensive layers.

TLS encryption protects data in transit between endpoints. Tokenization replaces sensitive card details with tokens that can be used in permitted contexts without exposing the original number to every downstream system. PCI DSS sets security requirements for environments that store, process or transmit cardholder data, with applicability depending on the merchant's implementation.

Fraud detection can examine transaction patterns, device signals and other legally permitted risk indicators. 3-D Secure and issuer controls add further checks where appropriate. None of these systems is infallible, and stronger controls can introduce checkout friction. Businesses have to balance conversion, user experience and risk.

A gateway integration should also enforce access control, protect API keys, verify incoming callbacks, log safely and avoid leaking card details through debugging or analytics systems.

What about UPI and other payment methods?

Not every digital payment follows the card-network pathway described above. In India, for example, UPI transactions use a different participant and messaging structure. A payment service may offer cards, UPI, net banking and wallets behind one merchant integration, but the underlying authorization, settlement, refunds and dispute processes are not interchangeable.

That is why a technical document should specify the payment rail it describes. “How payments work” is usually too broad to imply that every method behaves like a card payment.

A final note on refunds and disputes

A customer can receive a refund after a captured or settled purchase, depending on the provider and payment method. Cardholders may also dispute transactions, potentially leading to chargeback processes governed by network rules. These are separate post-payment workflows, not just the initial authorization in reverse.

Merchants should preserve transaction evidence, maintain clear refund policies and track the resulting balance adjustments. A sale is not always the end of the accounting story.

From checkout to the books

The useful mental model is three layers: the customer sees a checkout, the payment system handles authorization and financial processing, and the merchant's finance team confirms what actually arrived.

The two-second approval message is only the visible beginning. Clearing, settlement, payouts and reconciliation are what complete the story. Understanding that distinction helps developers design more reliable integrations and helps businesses understand why a successful payment and available cash are not always the same thing.
